How to Read Woodworking Plans and Cut Lists Like a Pro

A plan only saves you money and time if you read it correctly before the first cut. Most wasted lumber does not come from a bad saw cut — it comes from a plan that was misread at the start.

1. Nominal size vs. actual size

Lumber is sold by its nominal size, not its actual milled size. A "2x4" is actually 1.5 inches by 3.5 inches once it has been planed at the mill. A "1x6" is 0.75 inches by 5.5 inches. If a cut list gives you dimensions and you assume the nominal size is the real size, every joint in the project will be off by a quarter inch or more.

2. Board nesting and cut order

A cut list is only as efficient as its nesting diagram — the layout that shows how each piece fits onto a standard 8-foot or 10-foot board. A good plan groups cuts by length first, so you are not left with a full board's worth of unusable offcuts.

3. Grain direction and orientation marks

Cut lists that only give length and width, with no grain direction, are a warning sign. Grain direction affects strength, how a joint holds glue, and how the piece looks once finished. A complete plan marks grain direction on every structural or visible piece.

4. The exploded 3D view matches the cut list

Before cutting, count the pieces in the exploded diagram and compare that number to the cut list line items. A mismatch here — more parts in the drawing than in the list, or pieces 'left out' of the material counts — is a clear sign to slow down and verify the plan.

5. Hardware and fastener specs, not just wood

A cut list without a hardware list is incomplete. Screw length, pocket-hole size, hinge type, and glue type all affect the final joint strength. If the cut list doesn't tell you the hardware, the assembly is not fully specified.

Before you cut

  • Confirm actual lumber size vs nominal size.
  • Mark all grain direction before you rip.
  • Compare cut count with the exploded view.
