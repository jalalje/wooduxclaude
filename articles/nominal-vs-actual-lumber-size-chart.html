Nominal vs. Actual Lumber Sizes: The Chart Beginners Actually Need

Every piece of dimensional lumber is sold under a name that does not match its real size. A "2x4" has never measured 2 inches by 4 inches at the lumber yard. Once you know why, and the actual sizes, the confusion goes away for good.

Why the gap exists

Nominal size refers to the board's dimensions before it is dried and planed smooth at the mill. Both processes remove material — drying shrinks the wood slightly, and planing shaves the rough surfaces flat. What is left is the actual size, which is what you should measure with, ever, regardless of what the label at the store says.

Actual sizes are the U.S. industry standard and can vary slightly by species and mill. Always measure the specific board in hand for critical joinery.

Where this silently breaks a project

A plan that lists a dado as "3.5 inches wide for a 4x4 post" is already correct if it means actual size — but if a beginner reads that as 4 full inches and routs the dado to match, the post will rattle inside an oversized slot. The mistake is invisible until glue-up, which is exactly when it becomes expensive.

The one habit that prevents it

Measure the actual board with a tape measure before cutting any joint sized to it, even when a plan gives a number. Lumber varies slightly between suppliers and even between boards at the same yard, so a plan's stated actual size is a close estimate, not a guarantee.
