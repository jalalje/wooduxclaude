Workshop Setup

Building a Woodworking Workshop for Under $1,000

A commercial-grade shop is not a requirement for building solid furniture or outdoor structures. A working shop needs four things: a stable cutting method, a way to join pieces cleanly, a flat assembly surface, and a plan for dust and safety. Everything past that is a convenience, not a requirement.

Core tools first, machinery later

Four tools cover most beginner and intermediate projects without a stationary saw:

  • Circular saw with a track or guide: cuts sheet goods as accurately as a cabinet saw, at a fraction of the cost and footprint.
  • Cordless drill and impact driver: covers pilot holes, driving, and most joinery assembly.
  • Router with a pocket-hole jig: gives you clean, strong joints without a mortiser or dedicated joinery machine.
  • Mobile workbench: a flat, movable surface that folds away in a shared garage space.

Buy these four before anything else. A table saw or planer adds speed later, but neither replaces what these four tools already cover.

Layout order for a small space

Set up in this order: assembly bench in the center, cutting station along one wall with clearance for a full sheet of plywood, a dedicated tool wall or pegboard within arm's reach of the bench, and a dust collection point closest to whichever tool produces the most dust — usually the router or sander. In a garage shared with a car, mount the workbench on locking casters so the whole layout can move in ten minutes.

Dust collection on a budget

A shop vacuum with a cyclone separator lid handles most beginner dust loads for under $80 total. Attach it directly to the router or sander port when the tool supports it. Fine dust is the real long-term health risk in a small shop, so this is one of the few places worth spending before you strictly need to.

Electrical basics

Most garage circuits are rated for 15 or 20 amps. A circular saw, shop vacuum, and work light running at the same time can approach that limit. Spread high-draw tools across separate outlets where possible, and avoid daisy-chaining power strips — a tripped breaker mid-cut is inconvenient, but a shared extension cord under load is a fire risk.
