Woodworking Plans vs. Designing Your Own

A tested plan and a custom design solve different problems. Neither is the "right" answer in every case — the better question is which one fits the specific project, budget, and space you are working with.

Where a tested plan wins

  • Lumber cost: a checked cut list has already been nested for minimal waste, which a first custom design usually has not.
  • Structural load: shelving, benches, and anything load-bearing benefits from joinery and spans that have already been built and tested.
  • Time: skipping the design phase means more time at the saw and less at the drawing table.
  • First attempts at a new joint or technique: learning dovetails or mortise-and-tenon is easier against a plan that has already solved the layout problem.

Where designing your own wins

  • Non-standard spaces: a shelf or cabinet built to fit an exact alcove will always beat a generic plan resized after the fact.
  • Reused or reclaimed material: odd board widths and lengths from reclaimed lumber rarely match a plan's cut list.
  • Personal fit: bench and table heights, in particular, benefit from being sized to the people using them.
  • Once you already know the joinery: designing from scratch is far easier once you have built the same joint type from a plan at least once.

A practical middle path

Most experienced builders do not fully commit to one approach. A common pattern is to start from a tested plan and then adjust the dimensions — height, depth, or shelf count — while keeping the plan's joinery and cut-list logic intact. This keeps the structural and lumber-efficiency benefits of a tested design while still fitting your specific space.

If you are starting from a tested base to adjust later, the 16,000-plan library is organized by project type and size, so finding a close starting point is faster than designing from a blank page.
