Clamps for Woodworking: Which Type for Which Job

No single clamp handles every job in a woodworking shop. A clamp strong enough for a wide panel glue-up is the wrong tool for holding two small pieces square, and a corner clamp built for right angles is useless on a curved edge. Match the clamp to the job before you buy a full set of any one type.

Bar clamps — for wide glue-ups

Bar clamps run the length of a metal or composite bar and apply even pressure across a wide surface, making them the right choice for panel glue-ups — several boards edge-glued into a tabletop or door. Buy them in a length a few inches longer than your widest expected glue-up.

Spring and quick clamps — for fast, light-duty holding

Spring clamps and one-handed quick clamps apply light pressure fast, with no threading or tightening needed. Use them for holding a jig in place, keeping a small piece from shifting while you drill, or tacking a piece in position before a stronger clamp goes on. They don't apply enough pressure for a structural glue joint on their own.

Corner clamps — for square frames and boxes

A corner clamp holds two pieces at a fixed right angle while glue sets or you drive a fastener — built for picture frames, box corners, and face-frame assemblies where "square" matters more than raw clamping force.

Sash clamps — for long frame and door glue-ups

Sash clamps (also sold as pipe clamps, built on a length of pipe instead of a solid bar) extend further and apply more pressure than a standard bar clamp, making them the traditional choice for door and window frame glue-ups, and any panel wider than a standard bar clamp reaches.

How many clamps you actually need to start

A rule of thumb that holds up in practice: buy one more clamp than you think the current project needs. Running out of clamps mid-glue-up, with wet glue setting and no way to add pressure to the last joint, costs more than the price of one extra clamp.

Clamping pressure mistakes that warp a glue-up

Over-tightening squeezes too much glue out of the joint, weakening it, and can bow a panel if pressure isn't spread evenly across multiple clamps. Alternate clamps above and below the panel, and tighten in stages — snug all of them first, then go back and bring each to full pressure — rather than fully tightening one clamp before moving to the next.
