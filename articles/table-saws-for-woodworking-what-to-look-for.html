Table Saws for Woodworking: What to Look For

A table saw earns its space in a shop once accuracy, not raw cutting ability, becomes the limit on your projects. Before buying one, check the features that affect safety, precision, and long-term usefulness.

Benchtop, contractor, and cabinet saws

Table saws scale by size and power, roughly in this order: benchtop saws are portable and light enough to store between uses; contractor saws sit between portable and permanent; cabinet saws are bigger, heavier, and built for repeatable accuracy in a fixed shop.

Fence accuracy matters more than motor power

The rip fence controls how parallel your cut stays to the blade across the full length of a board. A fence that drifts out of parallel or doesn't lock securely can create expensive mistakes fast, even if the motor is strong.

Blade selection: general-purpose, rip, and crosscut

A general-purpose combination blade handles most beginner work. A rip blade, with fewer, larger teeth, cuts faster along the grain; a crosscut blade is better when you need cleaner cuts across the grain.

Safety features to check before buying

  • A blade guard that doesn't need to be removed for normal cuts
  • A riving knife, which sits behind the blade to stop the wood from pinching and kicking back
  • An anti-kickback pawl system, on saws that include one

Before you buy

  • Measure your shop floor and outfeed space.
  • Check fence alignment and lock repeatability.
  • Compare full-height riving knife and guard design.

Don't buy a saw missing these features to save money — a table saw is the single most dangerous common tool in a hobby shop, and these features are there to protect you and the work.

Space and power requirements

Measure your available floor space with the saw's full extension — outfeed and rip capacity — not just its folded footprint. Check the saw's motor size, the voltage it uses, and whether your circuit can support it.
