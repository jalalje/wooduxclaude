Wood Planers for Beginners: Hand Planes vs. Power Planers

A planer's job is to bring a board to a consistent thickness across its full length, not just to smooth the surface — sanding smooths, but it doesn't fix a board that's thicker on one end than the other. Hand planes and power planers solve that same problem at very different scales and price points.

What a planer actually does

A planer removes a thin, even layer of material with each pass, correcting a board's thickness and flattening its face. This matters most when you're milling rough lumber yourself or resawing a thick board on a band saw — pre-surfaced lumber from a supplier arrives already planed to a consistent thickness.

Hand plane types

  • Block plane — small, one-handed, for trimming end grain and easing edges
  • Jack plane — a general-purpose bench plane for flattening and initial stock removal
  • Smoothing plane — shorter and finer-set than a jack plane, for a final, glass-smooth surface pass

Hand planes take practice to set up and use well, but need no power, no dust collection, and cost far less than a power planer.

Benchtop power planers: what to check before buying

  • Cutting width, which sets the widest board you can plane in one pass
  • Depth of cut per pass, which affects how many passes a rough board needs
  • Snipe, a common flaw where the very ends of a board come out slightly thinner than the middle — check reviews and support the board's ends during the pass to reduce it

Do you need a planer at all as a beginner

If you're buying pre-surfaced lumber from a supplier, you don't need a planer yet — it's one of the tools worth skipping at the start; see Essential Woodworking Tools for Beginners for the rest of that list. A planer earns its place once you start milling rough lumber or resawing boards on a band saw.
